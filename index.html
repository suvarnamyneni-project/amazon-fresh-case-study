01 · Overview

A grocery store that starts with dinner

People shopping for groceries online in India wanted one place for three things: curated recipes, the ingredients those recipes need, and same-day delivery. That is the problem I designed for. Most grocery apps are organised the way a supermarket is: aisles, categories, brands. That works when you already have a list. It works less well when the real question is "what are we eating tonight?", because then the shopper has to choose a dish somewhere else, write down the ingredients, and search for each one.

Shop by Recipe puts that whole loop inside Amazon Fresh. Customers browse recipes from well-known chefs, pick how many people they are cooking for, leave out anything already in the kitchen, and send the rest to the cart in one tap. The recipes are curated for the range of food people actually cook in India, so trying something new does not mean leaving the store to figure out the shopping.

This page is the research and the design together: the competitor review, the survey, the personas, the flow, the information architecture, the wireframes, and then the screens. Every final screen is also linked in the clickable prototype.

02 · My role

What I owned

I was the designer on this feature during my time at Amazon. My work covered:

  • Framing the problem from the research: a survey of 48 people, a look at other grocery apps, and three personas.
  • The end-to-end journey, from the Fresh storefront to the cart, including the user flow and information architecture.
  • Information architecture for recipe discovery: shelves, search, filters and the Veg Only toggle.
  • Interaction and visual design for all seven screens, built on Amazon's existing mobile patterns, from sketches through wireframes to Figma.
  • The first-run explainer that shows new visitors how the feature works.
  • A high-fidelity, clickable prototype in Figma so the whole idea could be reviewed and demoed end to end, not just described.

I worked alongside product and engineering partners, and I used the prototype as the shared reference for walking through the flow and talking through trade-offs.

03 · Problem

The gap between "I want to cook this" and "it's in my cart"

Shoppers think in meals. The store was organised in products.

To cook a single dish from Fresh, a customer had to leave the app to find a recipe, translate it into a shopping list, then search for each ingredient one by one and guess the quantities. Every step was a chance to give up, or to finish the order somewhere else.

For customers

Too much work to go from an idea to a basket, and no help with what or how much to buy for the number of people eating. The survey later made this concrete: people regularly got partway through an elaborate dish, found they were short one or two ingredients, and had to go to the store again.

For the business

Fresh already offered a wide range and fast delivery, but it relied on shoppers arriving with a list. A recipe gives people a reason to start a shop and fills a basket with several items in one go.

What the research made clear

I did not want the feature to rest on a hunch about how people plan dinner. These are the points I designed around, and each one comes from the survey, the competitor review, or the personas further down the page.

  • People start from the meal. 67% of the people I surveyed find offline grocery shopping time consuming, and 58.3% find it difficult to choose recipes online.
  • A recipe is already a shopping list. The common story was missing one or two ingredients for an elaborate dish and making an extra trip.
  • How much to buy depends on who is eating. Ankit needs enough for one person and cannot afford waste. Sakshi shops for a party.
  • 95.8% said they would rather shop for recipe ingredients with 2-hour delivery. Fresh was the only grocery app in the set I reviewed that already delivered in two hours, so the feature belonged there.
04 · Competitive research

Who already sold recipes, and who could deliver them

I compared grocery apps on two things that mattered for this feature: whether you could shop from a recipe, and how fast the groceries arrived. Amazon Fresh was the only one with 2-hour delivery. BigBasket does have a recipe feature, bb cookbook, but it falls short on delivery time and on the experience of getting from a dish to a basket.

The survey is what made the delivery gap decisive. 95.8% of participants preferred shopping for recipe ingredients with 2-hour delivery. Fresh was the app that could actually offer that, so it was the right place to design the feature.

06 · Key insights

The missing ingredient is the whole problem

One thing people kept coming back to: when they cook something elaborate, they are often short one or two ingredients. Then they have to make another trip to the store. That single gap is what Shop by Recipe is for. You should be able to see the dish, see what you are missing, and have just those items arrive in time to cook.

The insight board summed up the rest of the survey like this:

  • Most people follow recipes online.
  • About half the people buy groceries from Amazon Fresh.
  • Most people have insufficient ingredients for the required recipe at least once in a week.
  • Almost everyone would like to shop ingredients from their favorite chef's recipes.
  • Most people choose to have more Quick Cooking and Calorie Conscious options.

08 · Goals

What the design had to do

🍳

Inspire

Help people decide what to cook, with recipes grouped the way they actually think: quick meals, healthy bites, breakfast, desserts. The survey is why Quick Cooking and healthy options are shelves, not a filter buried at the end.

🧺

Translate

Turn a recipe into a basket automatically, scaled to the serving size, while letting people skip what they already have. That is the answer to being short one or two ingredients.

🧭

Stay familiar

Fit inside the Amazon Fresh shell so it feels like part of the store, not a separate app, and reuse the cart, search, and filters people already know.

How I'd measure success

  • Recipe-to-cart conversion: how often viewing a recipe leads to its ingredients landing in the cart.
  • Basket completeness: whether shoppers get everything a dish needs, without missing items.
  • Repeat recipe shopping: whether people come back and shop from recipes again.
  • Time to build a cart: how quickly someone goes from choosing a dish to a ready basket.
09 · User flow

From the Fresh homepage to checkout

I mapped the flow before the visual design. Someone opens Amazon Fresh, chooses Shop by Recipe, and then either searches, filters, or picks a featured recipe. The recipe page is where they change the serving size and the ingredients. From there the ingredients go into the cart and they check out. They can also add a review, add another recipe, or keep shopping.

  1. Start on the Amazon Fresh homepage.
  2. Open Shop by Recipe.
  3. Search for a recipe, select one from the homepage, or use a filter to find it.
  4. Land on the recipe page. Adjust the serving size, modify which ingredients to shop, and add them to the cart. A review can be added from here.
  5. From the cart, add another recipe, continue shopping, or proceed to check out, and then exit.

10 · Information architecture

Two ways in, one recipe page

The architecture has two entrances from the Amazon Fresh homepage. Shop by Recipe opens the categories. Search looks up a food or a cuisine. Both can reach the recipe page, and from a recipe you can also skip straight to the ingredients and checkout.

  • Amazon Fresh HomePage
    • Shop by Recipe header image. Different categories of the recipes page open.
      • Choose a recipe. This connects across to the recipe page options.
      • Filter recipes, by category, cuisine, rating, and difficulty level.
      • Preference toggle: Veg Recipes or Non Veg Recipes.
    • Search. Will search a food item or a cuisine.
      • Click on a recipe. The recipe page opens.
        • Adjust serving size.
        • Add or remove ingredients as per choice.
        • Write a review.
        • Rate the recipe.
        • Share the recipe.
        • Save to wish list.
      • Filter recipes.
      • Directly shop ingredients, then proceed to checkout.
        • Exit the app.
        • Continue shopping.
11 · Wireframes

Sketches, then a wireframe tool, then Figma

I refined the screens over several passes, looking for a layout that did the job and still felt straightforward. I started with low-fidelity sketches, then built the app in a wireframe tool, and then moved into Figma, where I iterated on the screens and on the pieces inside them. The final screens in the next section are that Figma work. The sketches themselves were not a separate image on the project page. This is the wireframe set:

  • Homepage, with a choice of Shop by category or Shop by Recipe.
  • Shop by Recipe landing, with Chef's Special, Healthy Recipes, and Shop items on the cards.
  • Recipe page, with the image, share and wishlist, time, a serving stepper, ingredients, and Shop Ingredients.
  • The rest of the recipe page: directions, reviews, reviews with images, and rate this recipe.
  • Search, with a query, Veg Only, Filters, and results that show time and Shop items.
  • Shop items, with Buy Amazon Fresh Items, continue shopping, quantity, price, stock, and delete.
12 · Screen by screen

Design decisions

These are the Figma screens. Scroll inside each phone to see the full screen. The notes include the behaviour I specified for each one: the explainer, the veg toggle, the filters, the recipe page, and the cart.

Amazon Fresh landing
Screen 1 · Entry point

Meet shoppers where they already are

On the Fresh homepage you can start from the Shop by Recipe banner, or keep browsing the categories underneath it. Rather than hide the feature in a menu, I gave it the hero, with chef faces that signal "cooking" before you read a word.

Two actions in one bannerThe whole banner opens the recipe homepage for people ready to browse. "See how it works" is in the banner for people who want the model first. It opens a picture-and-text explainer of the steps.
A taste of recipes on the storefrontA Breakfast Recipes shelf sits below the offers, so the idea is visible even to people who scroll past the banner. Each card already has its own Shop items button.
Delivery promise up frontThe delivery address, "Delivery available Tuesday" and the 2-hour Prime Now badge stay at the top, because the value of a recipe drops if the ingredients arrive too late to cook. That was the gap the competitor review kept pointing at.
How it works
Screen 2 · Onboarding

Explain the model in four pictures

Buying from a recipe is a new idea for a grocery store, so "See how it works" opens a short, skimmable explainer instead of a tutorial. Pictures and a line of text, one step at a time.

One line per stepBrowse recipes by top chefs, choose a serving size, modify or shop the ingredients, get delivery in 2 hours. Each step pairs a short line with an illustration, alternating left and right so the eye zig-zags down the page.
End on the rewardThe page closes with "Enjoy your meal!" rather than a feature list, keeping the focus on the outcome.
Shop by Recipe homepage
Screen 3 · Discovery

Shelves by occasion, not by cuisine

The homepage is recipes from top chefs, for an Indian audience, arranged by the kind of meal you want. Quick Cooking and Healthy Bites are here because the survey asked for them. Breakfast, Chef Special, and Desserts sit alongside.

Cards that answer the key questions at a glanceEach card shows the dish photo, the chef's name, the rating and review count, a cooking-time badge, and a veg or non-veg marker, so people can compare without opening each recipe.
Veg Only as a first-class toggleThe veg / non-veg toggle sits next to Filters, not inside them. Switching it shows only that set. For many shoppers, vegetarian is a rule rather than a preference.
Filters for a narrower setFilters narrow the shelves to a category or another set the shopper cares about, without making them leave the homepage.
A shortcut for the decidedShop items on every card lets someone buy the ingredients directly and skip the recipe page. That is there for the person who already knows the dish.
WayfindingA "Shop by recipe" label replaces "Shop by category" in the Fresh tab bar, so people always know which mode of the store they are in.
Recipe search results
Screens 4–5 · Search

Search that feels like Amazon search

When someone already knows what they want, for example "chinese recipes", they search. The results can mix categories. I deliberately reused the standard Amazon results layout so nothing has to be learned.

List view for comparisonResults switch from shelves to a vertical list with a larger thumbnail, so time, rating and chef line up and are easy to scan down.
Filters that match the way recipes differVeg Only stays where it was on the homepage. Filters narrow the list by category, cuisine, difficulty, and customer rating.
Room for sponsored productsA sponsored placement sits above the results, keeping Amazon's ad inventory without breaking up the recipe list.

The Figma file has a second frame of this screen (v2) with the same layout and extra space at the bottom; both are in the prototype.

Recipe page
Screen 6 · Decision

Everything needed to commit to a dish

The recipe page is where browsing turns into buying. It opens with the title and a header image, and I ordered the rest by the questions people ask before they cook: can I make this, how long will it take, and what do I need?

Confidence, share, and saveCategory, rating, and an image carousel come first, with share and wishlist on the header. The cooking-time badge and the chef's name and photo follow, before any instructions.
Serving size drives the quantitiesA serving-size dropdown sits with difficulty and with prep, cooking, and total time. Choosing a serving size changes the ingredient quantities to match. Difficulty runs from Easy to Hard, and the time goes up as the dish gets harder.
An ingredient list that is also a checklistEvery ingredient is listed. A green tick means the item goes in the basket; a yellow plus means it was left out and can be added back. In the example, five of eight ingredients are ticked, and those five are exactly what show up in the cart. A full-width Shop Ingredients button closes the list, and there is a direct way to shop those ingredients from here.
Directions, then verified reviewsThe steps are written to be followed in order. After that, verified users can look at recipe photos, add their own, write a review, and rate the recipe. "Rate this recipe" closes the page.
Shop items
Screen 7 · Conversion

Land in the cart people already trust

Instead of building a special recipe checkout, I sent ingredients into the regular Amazon Fresh cart as real, branded products. The list is what was added from the recipe, each line with its quantity.

Real products, editableEach ingredient becomes a specific product with price, stock status, a quantity picker and Delete, so customers stay in control of brand and amount.
More than one recipe in the same cartI specified that someone can shop ingredients for more than one recipe before checkout, and change the quantities for each. The prototype shows one recipe's basket. The behaviour I was designing is that a second dish joins the same list, rather than starting a second order.
Clear next stepsThe subtotal, how much more is needed for free delivery, a primary "Buy Amazon Fresh Items" button and "Continue shopping on Amazon Fresh" make it easy to check out or keep adding.

14 · Outcomes

What the design enables

  • One tap from ingredients to cart. A whole recipe becomes a basket without a single manual search.
  • Clearer recipe discovery. Occasion-based shelves, search, filters, and the Veg Only toggle help people find a dish that fits the moment.
  • Fewer missed items. The ingredient checklist makes it obvious what is going in the basket and what has been left out, which is the extra-trip problem from the survey.
  • A reusable recipe card pattern. The same card, with chef, rating, time, diet marker and Shop items, works on the storefront, the recipe homepage and search.

Just as important, the clickable prototype made the concept easy to review and demo: anyone could tap through the full journey instead of imagining it from static screens.

15 · Reflection

What I took away

The research decided the shelves and the delivery promise. Quick Cooking is a homepage shelf because 73% asked for more of those meals, not because it looked good in a grid. 2-hour delivery is in the explainer because 95.8% wanted recipe shopping with that speed, and because no competitor on the chart could offer it.

Reusing patterns is a design choice, not a shortcut. The most useful decisions here were about what not to invent: the Fresh shell, the search results list and the standard cart. That left the new ideas, the shelves and the ingredient checklist, room to stand out. BigBasket already had a cookbook. The difference I was designing was that the recipe and the basket are the same trip.

The ingredient checklist does the most work. It is the interaction that turns a recipe into a personal basket, which is what Ankit needs when he is short the last ingredient after work, and what Sakshi needs when she is shopping for a party. It is the first thing I would put in front of shoppers in a usability test.

What I would still do differently. I would test that checklist with shoppers before the visual design gets this polished, to see whether the tick and the plus read as clearly as I think they do. The survey told me the problem. It did not watch anyone use the control. Working on this taught me that the most valuable thing a prototype does is start that conversation.

16 · Next steps

What I'd propose next

Two kinds of next step. The first is what I would learn with shoppers. The second is what I would add to the feature once the core loop is solid. I have wanted the second set since I designed this.

Usability-test the checklist

Put the ingredient checklist in front of shoppers to see whether the tick and plus states read clearly, and refine them based on what I learn.

"Already have it" toggle

Make the checklist pantry-aware, so staples a shopper usually skips, like oil or salt, can start out left out of the basket.

Serving-size scaling

Make the serving-size change update both the ingredient quantities and the basket, so the link between the two is visible while someone is deciding.

Random recipe picker

A spinning wheel for the night someone cannot decide. It gives the shelves a playful way to answer "you pick," and it is aimed at the 58.3% who already find choosing a recipe online difficult.

Voice assistant for the steps

Step-by-step directions you can hear, including on a HomePod, Google Home, or Echo, so the phone does not have to sit on the counter while you cook.

Recipe collections

Let people save recipes into their own groups, such as healthy recipes, kids' recipes, or desserts, and come back to them without searching again.
